**A puzzle and a deeper warning**

Not long ago I saw a very senior persion post online, genuinely baffled that an AI system had given them bad advice. How could something that sounded so intelligent, so composed, be so wrong? What were they supposed to make of that?

To me, the answer does not come from computer science at all, but from a different warning, one issued by psychiatrist and writer Iain McGilchrist in his book *The Master and His Emissary*. McGilchrist’s claim, backed by a great deal of clinical and neurological research, is that our culture has become increasingly dominated by a particular way of thinking associated with the brain’s left hemisphere: narrow, instrumental, obsessed with tools, procedures and abstractions. In the process, we are marginalising the very qualities that make us fully human: judgement, compassion, humour, the ability to live with ambiguity and still see the world clearly.

**Two hemispheres, one unbalanced culture**

The brain is split into two hemispheres. The caricature of “left logical, right creative” is misleading, and most popular science on this is woefully oversimplified. I am not going to pretend this short piece can do justice to McGilchrist’s scholarship; if you want the careful version, you should go to his book. But as a rough sketch, it is useful.

The right hemisphere, in his account, is the master: it maintains a broad, living sense of reality. It is more closely connected to the senses and to the external world. It is quite comfortable holding contradictions in mind, seeing wholes rather than just parts, and responding to nuance. It is where we find imagination, empathy, good humour and good judgement.

The left hemisphere is more like a toolkit. It is superb at manipulation in a very broad sense: manipulating symbols, tools, language, and the hands we use to act on the world. It likes clear rules, linear steps and well defined problems. It is intensely useful, but only when it is serving a larger understanding of the world rather than replacing it.

**The watchful rabbit**

Picture a rabbit grazing in in the woods. One part of its brain is focused on the mechanics of eating: locating plants, nibbling, chewing, swallowing. That is the left hemisphere at work, engaged in a tightly framed task. Meanwhile, the right hemisphere is on patrol: eyes, ears and nose scanning for novelty, a shadow, a rustle, a scent that might signal a fox or a dog. The right is not doing the efficient immediate task; it is doing something more important: maintaining a live connection to reality in all its unpredictability, because that is what keeps the rabbit alive.

Crucially, our interaction with the world tends to follow a pattern of right, then left, then right. First the right hemisphere receives and appraises the world: the rabbit smells the dog. Then the left takes over a specific, focused response: the rabbit runs, following a learned route to safety. Then control returns to the right, which reassesses the new situation, stays on alert and decides what happens next. The problem is that our culture has started to act as if the middle step were all that mattered.

**Dopamine, adrenaline, and digital life**

You can see the same split in how the hemispheres are fuelled and rewarded. The left hemisphere’s style of engagement is more heavily linked to dopamine, the neurotransmitter of reward, novelty and “just one more”. Our digital environment is finely tuned to that system: likes, notifications, infinite scroll. You get a quick little hit, then another, then another. The more you do it, the more you need in order to get the same effect. The right hemisphere’s engagement is more adrenaline based, not a rapid fire loop of small rewards but a sustained, alert presence. It can stay watchful for a long time without needing constant novelty.

Modern AI, particularly the large language models that have exploded into public consciousness, is an almost perfect expression of this left hemisphere style. Our main language centres sit in the left hemisphere, handling orderly, rule bound language: grammar, syntax, explicit reasoning. Quirkier forms of expression, silly noises, singing, nonsense words, draw more on the right. Large language models are, in essence, artificial language centres. We taught them to predict the next word in a sentence, to string sentences together in ways that look and feel like human prose. However, because language is our main tool for reasoning about the world, that simple training goal has produced systems that can now code, summarise, argue, draft contracts and more.

**Language, hands, and left brain tools**

Two things made that possible: computers became powerful enough to process vast amounts of data, and the rise of the web gave us great amounts of text to feed them. It is worth noting that in the brain, the language areas sit very close to the regions controlling fine hand movements. With our hands we manipulate the physical world; with language we manipulate the social and intellectual world. I find it intriguing that many words are hand metaphors: we handle a problem, grasp an idea, point something out. Tool use, whether with fingers or with phrases, is deeply left hemisphere territory. In that sense, large language models are a very pure embodiment of left brain strengths.
What they lack are the balancing strengths of the right.

The right hemisphere is capable of entertaining contradictions. It can see that something is both true and false at the same time, or true but incomplete. It is more visual, more imaginative, better at reading faces, contexts and atmospheres than texts. Above all, it is better at recognising when the current way of framing a situation is no longer valid, when the game has changed.

This is exactly where AI struggles. It will work tirelessly within a frame; it is not good at noticing when the frame itself is wrong.

**Context blindness: when the game has changed**

If you have used the new breed of “agentic” coding tools, you may have seen this in action. The system writes some code, runs the test suite, sees a failure, tweaks the code, runs the tests again, fails again, tweaks again. Sometimes, with enough iterations, it converges onto the fix. In my experience, perhaps one time in five it genuinely cracks a difficult problem. The rest of the time, it spirals off in the wrong direction, trapped in the same basic loop: edit, test, tweak; edit, test, tweak.

This is what I call context blindness. The tool is superb at exploring variations within the task it has been given. It is strikingly bad at standing back and saying: “Wait. Maybe this test is wrong. Maybe the requirements were mis-specified. Maybe the real problem is somewhere else entirely.” A rabbit that cannot be shaken out of its eating loop will not last long once the fox appears.

Humans, obviously, are not immune to that kind of blindness. However, we do at least possess a built in capacity to feel that something is off, to sense that the context has changed, and to switch modes. That is a right hemisphere gift.

**Can AI ever grow a right brain?**

Where, then, would an AI right brain come from? Large language models were trained on our textual output. Robots that can manipulate the physical world are now trained on oceans of recorded sensor data and scaled out simulated environments. But what exactly would you train good judgement on? What is the right learning signal for “not just answer the question, but notice when the question itself is misguided”? Where is the mass of data that truly captures ambiguity, irony, shifting social norms, moral nuance, the way that today is subtly but crucially different from yesterday?
We do not yet know. Until we do, the imbalance will remain. We are building ever more powerful left hemispheres with no corresponding right brain partner.

**You are the missing hemisphere**

This brings me back to that puzzled senior and the bad AI advice. The most helpful way to think about today’s AI, I would argue, is not as a standalone intelligence but as an extension of our left brain. It is astonishingly good at taking on the heavy lifting, symbol shuffling work that burns out our dopamine reserves: writing first drafts, crunching data, refactoring code, juggling schedules. Used well, that is a genuine productivity revolution.

In this relationship, you are the right brain. You are the one who supplies the wider sense of reality. You decide what the real question is, whether the framing makes sense, whether the answer, however eloquent, fits the world as you know it. You are the one with humour, compassion, moral instinct and skin in the game.

Once you see things that way, there is less temptation to anthropomorphise this magical box of cleverness. If it gives you bad advice, that is not because it is malicious, or even necessarily “stupid” in a human way. It is because it was operating in a context that did not match the one you care about. Left to itself, it has no reliable way to notice that mismatch and step out of the loop.

We do. So make use of these left brained clever boxes of tricks, but hold on to your wider awareness, your scepticism, your sense of proportion, your humanity.

AI does not have those. That is why, astonishing as it is, the intelligence that matters most is still yours.
